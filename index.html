What SimpleKit is

SimpleKit is a growing library of free financial calculators and planning tools for Canadians. Instead of treating each money decision like a completely separate problem, the homepage acts as a starting point for the questions that tend to connect in real life: housing affects cash flow, cash flow affects debt payoff, debt payoff affects savings capacity, and savings capacity affects retirement choices. The tools are designed to be practical first, which means they focus on decisions people actually make at the kitchen table, on a lunch break, or while comparing scenarios before a major purchase.

That includes mortgage planning, house affordability, rent versus buy comparisons, debt payoff planning, budgeting, take-home pay estimates, retirement planning, CPP timing, RRSP versus TFSA choices, investment growth, fees, net worth tracking, and a handful of supporting tools for recurring planning tasks. The goal is not to overwhelm you with every possible metric. The goal is to help you understand enough to move forward with more confidence.

Who these tools are for

The tools are especially useful for Canadians who want a better handle on everyday money decisions without jumping straight into advisor jargon or spreadsheet overload. Some people arrive with a narrow question like “How much house can I realistically afford?” Others are trying to understand a bigger planning picture such as whether they should focus on debt, build savings faster, or revisit a retirement target. SimpleKit is built for both kinds of users.

If you are early in your planning, the tools give you a low-friction place to begin. If you are further along, they help you compare scenarios, pressure-test assumptions, and connect one decision to the next. Everything is written in plain English so you can move from confusion to a workable plan without feeling like you need a finance credential just to get started.

Canadian Planning Use Cases

Common money decisions where a good calculator can save time, reduce stress, and improve the quality of the next step.

When you are trying to decide, not just calculate

The most helpful calculator is often the one that clarifies the decision underneath the numbers. A mortgage payment estimate matters because it affects your budget. A debt payoff model matters because it changes your timeline to start saving. A retirement estimate matters because it helps you see whether your current contributions, account mix, and CPP expectations are pointing in the same direction. SimpleKit is strongest when you use the homepage as a hub: begin with the broad question, pick the right category, and then move into the next tool only if that next question actually matters.

That is also why the site includes both calculators and educational guides. Sometimes the blocker is not the math. It is understanding the decision well enough to know which numbers matter. A short guide on CPP timing, retirement planning basics, or RRSP versus TFSA tradeoffs can make the next calculator far more useful because you are entering it with the right frame.

Why this matters for Canadian households

Canadian financial planning has its own recurring pressure points: different provincial tax realities, CPP decisions, RRSP and TFSA tradeoffs, housing affordability, mortgage payment stress, debt-to-income questions, and the challenge of balancing today’s cost of living with longer-term goals. A generic calculator can still be useful, but a better planning experience recognizes the types of questions Canadians actually ask when they are trying to get more stable, buy a home, pay off debt, or work toward retirement.

SimpleKit is not trying to replace professional advice or pretend one estimate answers everything. It is designed to give you a clearer first model, help you compare scenarios, and reduce the friction of getting oriented. For many people, that first layer of clarity is what turns a vague intention into a practical next step.

Why calculators help

Good planning gets easier when you can compare scenarios instead of relying on rough guesses.

Financial calculators are useful because they turn “I think this might work” into “here is what changes if I pay more, save longer, borrow less, or delay the decision.” That does not remove uncertainty, but it usually makes the next step clearer.

Use one tool to answer the immediate question

If you only need to compare a few scenarios, a focused calculator is often enough. That is especially true for debt payoff, savings goals, emergency funds, mortgage payments, and credit card payoff estimates.

Use connected tools when the decision spans multiple categories

Big decisions like buying a home, changing contract work, or updating a retirement plan often touch income, cash flow, debt, and savings at the same time. In those cases, the homepage works best as a hub for the next few steps, not just a single click.
