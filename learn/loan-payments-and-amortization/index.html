Loan Payments and Amortization

A loan payment is the result of several moving parts: the amount borrowed, interest rate, repayment period, and payment schedule. Amortization shows how those payments gradually shift from interest toward principal.

Understand what drives the payment

Principal is the amount being repaid

A larger starting balance generally means a larger payment or a longer repayment period when the rate is unchanged.

Interest is the price of borrowing

A higher interest rate raises the borrowing cost and usually the required payment. The exact result depends on how the loan compounds and how often payments are made.

Longer amortization lowers the payment but raises total interest

Stretching repayment over more periods can make each payment easier to carry while keeping the balance outstanding—and accruing interest—for longer.

Extra payments shorten the schedule

When an extra payment goes to principal without a penalty, future interest is calculated on a smaller balance and the payoff date can move forward.

Read an amortization schedule

Interest and principal change over time

With a typical level-payment loan, early payments contain more interest because the balance is larger. As principal falls, more of the same payment can go toward principal.

Outstanding balance is the key checkpoint

The balance after each payment shows how quickly the debt is actually disappearing and makes different repayment strategies easier to compare.

Check your actual contract

Fees, variable rates, payment rules, prepayment restrictions, and compounding conventions can make a real loan differ from a general calculator.
